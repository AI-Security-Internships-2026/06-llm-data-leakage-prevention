## Paper Summaries (Week 1 & 2)

### Paper 1 — Extracting Training Data from LLMs

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models |
| **Authors** | Carlini, Tramèr, Wallace, Jagielski, Herbert-Voss, Lee, Roberts, Brown, Song, Erlingsson, Oprea, Raffel |
| **Year** | 2021 |
| **Venue** | USENIX Security 2021 |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07805 |
| **Method** | Generated text from GPT-2 at scale, then used membership inference to identify memorized training examples; introduced the "exposure" metric to quantify memorization |
| **Dataset** | GPT-2 (117M–1.5B parameters) trained on WebText (internet-scraped data) |
| **Key result** | Successfully extracted 604 memorized training examples including names, phone numbers, email addresses, and verbatim text passages |
| **Limitation** | Requires a large number of model queries; focused on unconditional text generation rather than chat/instruction-tuned models |
| **Relevance to our project** | Foundational paper proving LLMs memorize and leak training data verbatim — the core motivation for our entire project; directly cited by garak's leakreplay probe |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> The key finding: larger models memorize more, and data that appears multiple times in training is far more likely to be extracted.

### Paper 2 — Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | Quantifying Memorization Across Neural Language Models |
| **Authors** | Carlini, Ippolito, Jagielski, Lee, Tramèr, Zhang |
| **Year** | 2022 |
| **Venue** | ICLR 2023 |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07646 |
| **Method** | Systematic study measuring memorization as a function of model size, training data duplication count, and context length using a k-extractability definition |
| **Dataset** | Multiple LLMs of varying sizes trained on The Pile |
| **Key result** | Memorization scales predictably with model size and data repetition — a sequence seen 10× in training is ~10× more likely to be extractable |
| **Limitation** | Focuses on extractability from unconditional generation; doesn't address chat-mode or RAG leakage |
| **Relevance to our project** | Provides the theoretical framework for understanding why leakage happens; informs how we should weight risk levels in our detector — repeated data patterns are higher risk |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> Memorization is not a binary property — it exists on a spectrum and is directly influenced by training data duplication.

### Paper 3 — Scalable Extraction from Production LLMs

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | Scalable Extraction of Training Data from Production Language Models |
| **Authors** | Nasr, Carlini, Hayase, Jagielski, Cooper, Ippolito, Choquette-Choo, Wallace, Tramèr, Lee |
| **Year** | 2023 |
| **Venue** | arXiv preprint |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17035 |
| **Method** | Divergence attacks — special tokens (e.g. repeating a word forever) cause the model to exit chat/RLHF mode and fall back to raw training data generation |
| **Dataset** | Production LLMs including ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-turbo) |
| **Key result** | Extracted gigabytes of training data from ChatGPT including personal information, despite alignment and RLHF fine-tuning |
| **Limitation** | Requires specific divergence-inducing prompts; may be patched by providers after disclosure |
| **Relevance to our project** | Proves that even production-hardened, RLHF-aligned models leak training data — output-side detection (our approach) is necessary even when input guardrails exist |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> RLHF fine-tuning does not prevent memorization leakage — it only suppresses it under normal prompting conditions.

### Paper 4 — ProPILE: Probing Privacy Leakage in LLMs

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | ProPILE: Probing Privacy Leakage in Large Language Models |
| **Authors** | Kim, Woo, Kim, Choi et al. |
| **Year** | 2023 |
| **Venue** | NeurIPS 2023 |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01881 |
| **Method** | Black-box probing — partial PII (e.g. a name) is provided as context to check if the model completes with associated private data (email, phone, address) |
| **Dataset** | The Pile dataset; Enron email corpus |
| **Key result** | LLMs consistently leak associated PII when given partial identifying information; association-based leakage is more dangerous than verbatim extraction |
| **Limitation** | Black-box only; doesn't analyze internal model representations |
| **Relevance to our project** | Directly demonstrates the PII leakage scenario our detector targets; shows that entity-association (not just verbatim match) must be detected — motivates going beyond simple regex |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> Providing a person's name alone is often sufficient to extract their email address from a model trained on public data.

### Paper 5 — Analyzing PII Leakage in Language Models

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | Analyzing Leakage of Personally Identifiable Information in Language Models |
| **Authors** | Lukas, Salem, Sim, Tople, Wutschitz, Zanella-Béguelin |
| **Year** | 2023 |
| **Venue** | IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy (S&P) 2023 |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00539 |
| **Method** | Systematic taxonomy of PII leakage types: verbatim memorization, inference-based leakage, and association leakage; proposes evaluation framework for each |
| **Dataset** | Enron email corpus; synthetic PII-injected datasets |
| **Key result** | Three distinct PII leakage categories exist — verbatim, inference, and association — each requiring different mitigation strategies |
| **Limitation** | Taxonomy is based on current model architectures; may not fully apply to future architectures |
| **Relevance to our project** | Provides the theoretical vocabulary for classifying leakage types; our detector currently handles verbatim PII (Presidio) — this paper motivates extending to inference-based leakage in later weeks |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> Verbatim memorization is only one of three distinct privacy risks — inference and association leakage are often harder to detect and prevent.

### Paper 6 — Deduplicating Training Data Mitigates Privacy Risks

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | Deduplicating Training Data Mitigates Privacy Risks in Language Models |
| **Authors** | Lee, Ippolito, Nystrom, Zhang, Eck, Callison-Burch, Carlini |
| **Year** | 2022 |
| **Venue** | ICML 2022 |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06539 |
| **Method** | Near-duplicate detection and removal from training corpora before model training using suffix arrays and MinHash |
| **Dataset** | The Pile, C4, LM1B datasets |
| **Key result** | Deduplication reduces extractable memorization by up to 10× with minimal impact on model perplexity or downstream task performance |
| **Limitation** | A training-time mitigation only — does not help with already-deployed models; near-duplicates may still leak |
| **Relevance to our project** | Training-time deduplication is a complementary prevention strategy to our inference-time detection; understanding both layers is essential for a complete prevention pipeline |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> Data that appears only once in training is orders of magnitude less likely to be memorized than data that appears hundreds of times.

### Paper 7 — Preventing Verbatim Memorization Gives False Sense of Privacy

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | Preventing Verbatim Memorization in Language Models Gives a False Sense of Privacy |
| **Authors** | Ippolito, Tram, Choquette-Choo, Carlini, Zhang |
| **Year** | 2023 |
| **Venue** | arXiv preprint |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08446 |
| **Method** | Demonstrates that blocking verbatim output reproduction (e.g. exact-match filters) does not prevent semantic leakage — models paraphrase memorized content |
| **Dataset** | GPT-2 and GPT-Neo models; manually verified leaked examples |
| **Key result** | Models can reproduce private training data in paraphrased form even when verbatim reproduction is blocked, bypassing naive output filters |
| **Limitation** | Focuses on generation models; less studied for instruction-tuned/chat models |
| **Relevance to our project** | Critical insight for our detector — Presidio-style regex/NER catches verbatim PII but not paraphrased leakage; motivates adding semantic/LLM-as-judge layer in later weeks |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> An exact-match output filter gives a false sense of security — the model can reveal the same private information in different words.

### Paper 8 — Beyond Memorization: Privacy Via Inference

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | Beyond Memorization: Violating Privacy Via Inference with Large Language Models |
| **Authors** | Staab, Vero, Balunović, Fischer, Vechev |
| **Year** | 2023 |
| **Venue** | arXiv preprint (ICLR 2024) |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298 |
| **Method** | Prompted GPT-4 to infer sensitive personal attributes (location, income, health) from Reddit comments without the model having memorized those specific facts |
| **Dataset** | Reddit PersonalReddit dataset with ground-truth labels |
| **Key result** | LLMs correctly infer sensitive personal attributes at rates far above chance — privacy violation through reasoning, not memorization |
| **Limitation** | Requires high-capability models; less effective on smaller models |
| **Relevance to our project** | Expands our threat model beyond memorized data — LLMs can construct new PII through inference; pure output scanning may miss this entirely |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> A model need not have memorized your data to violate your privacy — it can infer sensitive facts about you from unrelated context.

### Paper 9 — Quantifying Privacy Risks of Masked Language Models

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | Quantifying Privacy Risks of Masked Language Models Using Split Shadow Training |
| **Authors** | Mireshghallah, Uniyal, Wang, Evans, Berg-Kirkpatrick |
| **Year** | 2022 |
| **Venue** | EMNLP 2022 |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.09928 |
| **Method** | Adapts likelihood ratio membership inference attacks to masked language models (BERT-style) using split shadow training to estimate per-sample privacy risk |
| **Dataset** | MIMIC-III clinical notes; SST-2 sentiment dataset |
| **Key result** | BERT-style masked language models have significant membership inference vulnerabilities, especially when fine-tuned on small sensitive datasets |
| **Limitation** | Computationally expensive; requires training multiple shadow models |
| **Relevance to our project** | Shows leakage is not limited to autoregressive models — relevant if our pipeline is extended to encoder-based models for PII classification |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> Fine-tuning on small domain-specific datasets dramatically increases membership inference risk even for masked models.

### Paper 10 — The Secret Sharer: Evaluating Unintended Memorization

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Full title** | The Secret Sharer: Evaluating and Testing Unintended Memorization in Neural Networks |
| **Authors** | Carlini, Liu, Erlingsson, Kos, Song |
| **Year** | 2019 |
| **Venue** | USENIX Security 2019 |
| **URL / DOI** | https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08232 |
| **Method** | Inserts canary sequences (random credit card-like numbers) into training data, then measures how easily they can be extracted — defines the "exposure" metric |
| **Dataset** | Penn Treebank text corpus; custom canary-injected datasets |
| **Key result** | Even rare sequences seen only once in training can be memorized and extracted with high confidence; exposure grows with model capacity |
| **Limitation** | Canary-based evaluation may not reflect real-world sensitive data distribution |
| **Relevance to our project** | Introduces the "exposure" metric — the standard measure for quantifying leakage risk in our evaluation phase (Week 7); foundational methodology paper |

**Notes / Quotes:**
> A model that memorizes a single secret inserted once into billions of training tokens is a serious privacy risk — and this happens routinely.
